Case 01

Sonar started as my bachelor thesis. Two years later it's the way into the platform.

It began as a desktop-only proof of concept. It's now six ear-training games with a progression system on top, and the reason a producer would come to Sinko in the first place.

Role: research, UX, UI, frontend 2 years Wix Studio, Velo, vanilla JS

This case is about why Sonar makes people subscribe. Case 02 is about how the subscription itself was built.

01 / 05
Chapter 01

The prototype worked, and that was about all it did.

I built it for my bachelor thesis at UiA, on top of a prototype from an earlier course. It ran on desktop only, because the platform's audio handling wouldn't survive mobile, and each game was split across two iframes. Due to this, the game was desktop only for around a year before the redesign.

Original thesis build beside the current interface
Chapter 02

Rebuilding all six games for mobile.

I rebuilt all six as self-contained iframes on one shared message contract, which is what made mobile possible at all. It wasn't a port. A step sequencer and a piano roll don't survive being shrunk, so every game got its layout redesigned around touch.

The design decision

The beat sequencer game was the most difficult to adapt to mobile since the interface is naturally very wide. Solved by making the scrolling lock to each bar to make navigation simpler

Desktop layout and its mobile redesign, side by side

People were giving up in round two.

Chapter 03

None of the retention design matters if people leave in the first two minutes. I watched a tester who wasn't a musician get frustrated early on, because he was convinced he had no chance of hearing the difference. Once he understood that guessing wrong is how the thing works, he was getting them right by round three.

So the product had to say that before anyone else did. I rewrote the feedback so a wrong answer reads as the ear getting more precise, cut the onboarding from a playable tutorial down to a short sequence that just sets expectations, and gave experienced producers a way to skip the slow start.

What I rejected

A full detailed tutorial was considered but was scrapped in favor of simpler expectation management in the landing page

Onboarding scenes and the reframed feedback copy
Chapter 04

Progression had to hold together on its own.

XP is earned per session against a weekly cap, and anything over the cap gets banked rather than burned, because taking progress away punishes the exact behaviour I want more of. On top of that sit streaks, a rewards ladder with tier-based level caps, and a multiplier that separates Plus from Studio. Weak Spot sessions look at your two worst games and build a playlist out of them, so what you've earned decides what you practise next.

Why it matters

This was the feature that jumpstarted the subscription system as it gave a key trigger to convert from free players to paid subscribers

XP ladder, weekly cap and banked overflow
Chapter 05

The first ad campaign went badly.

It pointed paid traffic at the homepage, and the traffic bounced. The homepage asks you to work out what Sinko is, and someone arriving from an ad has exactly one thing in mind. So Sonar got its own way in: a dedicated landing page, three playable rounds before anyone signs up, and the rest behind a plan.

The part I like is the loop. The rewards are real Sinko products, so practising makes the store worth more to you, and the store makes practising worth more.

Landing page funnel and the demo-to-signup moment
Still open

What I'd still change.

I'd probably replace the tempo game with a chord-progression trainer, because that's something producers use every day. And Sonar hasn't launched yet, so the retention numbers are designed rather than proven. If the numbers point to that game being less useful or a common drop-off spot, I'll swap it out for a different game rather than guessing.

The system underneath all of it.

Every surface on Sinko inherits the same tokens, so building a new page is mostly assembly. The system keeps everything recognisable, and where a product needs its own character it breaks the rules on purpose rather than by accident.

Paper, ink, one accent

Light by default. Dark only where a section has to stand out, never just for atmosphere. Red is kept for things you can click.

The corner tells you what it is

Six steps from 6px to 48px, plus pill. Buttons are always pill and badges are pill chips, so the curve does some of the labelling for me.

Flat wherever it has to be read

Gradients live on product visuals and heroes. Anything you have to actually read stays flat, so the surface isn't competing with the words on it.

Sonar breaks the rules on purpose

Purple, after trying blue (too literal) and red (the brand accent, far too heavy once you're playing). A game shouldn't feel like the shop it's sitting in.

Case 02

Sinko was a handful of products that had nothing to do with each other.

Sample packs, plugins, beats and ear training were all sold separately, and nothing gave a producer a reason to stay. This was the work of designing a subscription that turned them into one thing.

Role: UX, UI, frontend, pricing structure Wix Studio, Velo, CMS

What was wrong

Someone could buy five different things from us and still not be able to say what Sinko was.

Three tiers, one sentence each

Core, Plus and Studio each had to make sense in about ten seconds, on every page they unlock.

You have to be able to see what you're getting

Credits, member discounts, loyalty cashback and the monthly Vault rotation. None of it does anything if you can't see what you already have.

What I can't prove yet

It hasn't launched, so I have no conversion numbers.

Case 03

A team project, on a problem I didn't pick.

Buddy is a two-sided bidding platform for the Norwegian housing market, built as a student venture project. I ran design and prototyping, with four teammates and a set of constraints I had no part in choosing.

Role: design and prototyping Team of 5 UiO venture project

The hard part

One bid card has to work for buyers, realtors and banks at the same time, and the stakes are different for every one of them. The first version focused mostly on consumers and research showed the need to improve the realtor experience was essential for the product to work

What changed

A two-column grid, and a button hierarchy where how heavy something looks matches what it actually does, so the action you can't undo never looks like the safe one.

Design isn't always the thing that wins

This project proved very concretely that something that looks nice isn't going to sell. The functionality needs to be supported by well though out design to succeed.

How I work.

Four things I've ended up believing, each of which cost me something.

01

The most useful tester I had wasn't a producer.

He was outside the target audience entirely, and he failed in a way none of my assumptions had predicted. Fixing it changed the first minute of the product.

02

Building it myself changes what I draw.

If I design something awkward, I'm the one who has to sit there building it.

03

I'd rather fix the token than the screen.

Defining the design system made building new pages and products a lot easier and more cohesive

04

I'd rather say I don't know yet.

Transparency is important in my work so if I have to show metrics, I would rather show what I have instead of making claims I can't back yet

Where AI fits.

I use it every day, and not as a shortcut, but as an accelerator on work I already know how to do.

Structuring the work

Before I start, I use it to break something vague into an order I can actually follow. Working out what to do first is where I used to lose most of the day.

Rough draft to finished

I write the first version myself, roughly, then use it to get to something I'd show someone. The idea is still mine. Getting it presentable just takes less time.

Where I stop

I don't use it to build things I couldn't build myself. If I can't read what comes back and say why it works, it doesn't ship.
